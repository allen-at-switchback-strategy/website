Brand Voice & Style
We work with you like a familiar trail guide: friendly, polite, caring, confident. We take on the unknown with excitement, we are prepared to act, and we show up when you call for help.

01 — THE STANCE
The familiar trail guide
A guide has ridden this terrain before, but not with you, and not today. So they set expectations honestly, name the hard turns before you reach them, and keep moving. They are glad you came. They are not impressed with themselves. Every sentence we write should sound like it came from someone standing beside you, pointing at the trail — not from a vendor across a table.
Friendly and polite
Warm, plain, unhurried. We greet people, use their names, and say thank you. Politeness is never formality — no "per my last email," no third person.
Caring
We write about the person's problem, not our product. We name the cost they are carrying before we name the fix, and we never make anyone feel behind for asking.
Confident
We recommend, we don't survey. Confidence sounds like specificity and a stated next step — never like superlatives, and never like certainty we haven't earned.
Excited by the unknown
A problem nobody has solved is the good part of the job. We say so, then immediately say how we'd find out — enthusiasm always arrives attached to a plan.
02 — CALIBRATION
We are this, not that
Each attribute has a failure mode on both sides. The left column is the target; the right is the drift to catch in edit.
ATTRIBUTE

Friendly
Warm and direct. "Good to meet you — send the export whenever it's easy and I'll take it from there."
Chummy or performative. No exclamation points, no "Hey folks!", no jokes at the client's expense.
Polite
Respectful of time and effort. We ask, we don't assign. We say what we need and why it helps.
Stiff or corporate. Passive voice, legalese, and "kindly advise" all read as distance, not manners.
Caring
Attentive to consequences — the team's workload, the budget, the person whose name is on the result.
Soft on the truth. Care is not agreeing with a bad plan, and it is never flattery.
Confident

Boastful or absolute. No "revolutionary," no "guaranteed," no claim we can't put a number and a source on.
Excited

Hype. Excitement about the unknown is curiosity, not adjectives — the moment it needs "game-changing," cut it.
Prepared
Every message ends knowing what happens next, who owns it, and by when.
Rigid. A plan we won't revise isn't preparation. We name what would change our mind.
03 — TONE BY SITUATION
Same voice, different weather
The attributes don't change by situation — the proportions do. These five cover most of what we write.
FIRST CONTACT
Lead with their terrain
Name the problem in their words before anything about us. One credential, maximum. End with a small, easy next step.
SCOPING THE UNKNOWN
Excited, then specific
Say plainly what we don't know yet, then how we'd learn it, what it costs, and how long. Curiosity always arrives with a method.
BAD NEWS
Front of the message
What happened, what it costs, what we're doing about it, what we need from you. No cushioning paragraph, no passive voice hiding the actor.
TEACHING
Nobody is behind
Define the term the first time and move on. Never "as you probably know" or "obviously." A guide explains the switchback without commenting on your fitness.
SOMEONE ASKS FOR HELP
Answer first, qualify second
Help arrives before terms do. Give the useful answer, then say what a real engagement would take. We never hold a small answer hostage to a big contract.
04 — VOICE IN ACTION
Three rewrites
The fastest way to hear the voice is to hear what it replaces. Left is the drift; right is the target.
OFF VOICE — HYPE
"Unlock transformative value and unleash your team's potential with our cutting-edge agentic AI solutions."
ON VOICE — SPECIFIC
"Your campaign cycle runs three weeks and four handoffs. Let's find out which of those handoffs an agent can carry, then build that one."
OFF VOICE — HEDGING
"It's difficult to say whether that would work in your environment without significant further discovery."
ON VOICE — HONEST AND READY
"I haven't built this against your CDP before, so I don't know yet. Here's how I'd find out in a week — and what I'd tell you if the answer is no."
OFF VOICE — COLD
"Per my previous email, the requested data export remains outstanding and is blocking delivery."
ON VOICE — HELPFUL
"I'm still waiting on the export to start Stage 02. Happy to hop on a call and pull it with your team if that's faster."

THE TRAIL METAPHOR — HANDLE WITH CARE
The trail language is structural, not decorative. It names real things: a switchback is a change of direction that still climbs, a Trailmap is the phased plan we sketch together, a grade is engagement difficulty, a stage is a phase of work.
One metaphor per passage. If a paragraph already has a switchback in it, the next sentence uses ordinary words.
Never stack them. "Let's summit this switchback and ride the singletrack to the peak" is unusable. Pick one, drop the rest.
Never in bad news. When something breaks, we speak in plain business terms. Metaphor there sounds like evasion.
It must survive translation. If replacing the trail word with the literal word loses no meaning, the metaphor was decoration — cut it.
06 — MECHANICS
House style
Person
"I" for the "guides" own work and opinions. "We" for the firm's method and commitments. "You" for the client, always — never "clients" or "organizations" when we mean the reader.
Sentences
Active voice, named actor. Contractions welcome. One idea per sentence; vary the length. Sentence case for headings, mono uppercase for labels only.
Numbers
Numerals throughout, always with a unit and a source: "3 weeks," "$6M annual savings," "4–8 wks." Spell out only at the start of a sentence. Never a stat we can't attribute.
Punctuation
Oxford comma. Spaced em dash — like this — for the aside. En dash for ranges. No exclamation points, no ellipses for drama, no ALL CAPS in prose.
HEADLINES
A claim or an invitation, under 9 words, no colon-subtitle construction. "One free session. Bring your messiest problem."
BUTTONS
Verb plus object, 2–4 words, uppercase. "BOOK A SESSION," "SEE THE TRAIL MAP." Never "Learn more," never "Submit."
ERRORS & EMPTY STATES
Say what happened and what to do, in that order. Never blame the user, never apologize twice. "Tell us where you ride — company name is required."

State it plainly, once, in ordinary prose — no trail metaphor draped over it — and attach the standard line: "Switchback Strategy is an independent consultancy and is not affiliated with, sponsored by, or endorsed by [vendor]." This applies to proposals, case studies, and the site footer alike.
THE EDIT TEST
Would a guide say this, standing next to you?
If it needs a superlative, cut it. If it hides who acts, rewrite it. If it doesn't end knowing what happens next, it isn't finished.
